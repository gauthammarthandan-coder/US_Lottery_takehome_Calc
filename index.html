What's your jackpot really worth?

It depends where you live in the US.

Win a $200M jackpot in the UK, Canada or Australia and you'd keep every penny — they don't tax lottery prizes. In the US you can't: the federal government takes ~37% no matter where you live. And then your state can pile on more — nothing in Texas, but millions in New York. This study shows two things for every state: how much of your jackpot vanishes to tax, and how far what's left actually goes where you live.

Methodology & sources

How we calculated this

The jackpot figure. Advertised jackpots are the annuity value — the full amount paid over 30 graduated annual payments. Most winners take the lump-sum "cash option" instead, which is roughly 48% of the headline (it varies with interest rates and by game). All figures here use that cash value: a $200M advertised jackpot is modelled as ≈$96M cash.

The tax. We apply the top federal rate of 37% to the cash value (held constant for every state, since federal tax is the same nationwide), then subtract each state's top tax rate on lottery winnings. Nine states take nothing. New York City and Yonkers add a local tax on top of the state rate. At smaller prizes the effective federal rate is lower than 37% and the lottery only withholds above $5,000 — so these figures describe large jackpots, where the top rate applies.

"Real value" (Ranking 2). Take-home dollars don't buy the same amount everywhere. We divide each state's after-tax take-home by its cost-of-living index (national average = 100) to get purchasing-power-adjusted value. This is what ranks the states — and it's why the nine no-tax states don't tie: a dollar stretches further in a cheaper state.

What the money buys / costs. The two tabs translate the dollars into local terms. What your winnings can buy (your take-home): homes you could buy outright, acres of land, and years you could live without working (take-home ÷ local annual living cost). What the tax cost you (the total lost): years of rent, years of childcare, full four-year college degrees funded, and years of an average local salary. A four-year degree is counted as a complete cost per person (tuition × 4), since it's a one-off, not an ongoing bill.

Sources. State tax rates: 2026 published state withholding/income-tax data. Income, rent & home values: U.S. Census Bureau ACS and Zillow/NAR 2026. Cost-of-living index and local annual spend: MERIC / World Population Review 2026 (US average = 100). Tuition: College Board / IPEDS. Childcare: Child Care Aware of America. Land values: USDA / state agricultural land averages. International tax treatment: national tax authorities (the UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and others do not tax lottery prizes).

Limitations. Every figure is an estimate for illustration. Real tax depends on filing status, deductions, other income and professional advice. Cash-value percentages move with interest rates. Cost-of-living, childcare and tuition averages vary within a state. The rankings are the same for Powerball and Mega Millions because state tax and cost of living don't depend on which game you win.
